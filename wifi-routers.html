01 / The landscape

The landscape

A consumer router is a WiFi system-on-chip, a set of RF front ends and antennas, some flash and RAM, an ethernet switch, and an amount of firmware entirely out of proportion to the size of the box. The silicon is not from here. The WiFi SoC will almost always be Broadcom, Qualcomm, MediaTek or Realtek, American and Taiwanese companies, and no delta house has displaced them. What Shenzhen owns is everything above that line: the integration, the antenna and thermal design, the firmware and cloud stack, the cost engineering, and the manufacturing at a scale nobody else offers. It is also worth knowing that the retail router in a box is the smaller half of this business. The larger half is carrier CPE, the gateways and optical terminals your internet provider hands you, bought in millions by telecoms operators who never put a brand on the outside.

That gives the delta two distinct trades. There are the retail brands, TP-Link and Tenda, competing on shelves and marketplaces worldwide, and there is the carrier ODM layer, which is genuinely enormous and almost invisible: Shenzhen Gongjin, trading as T&W, runs more than ten thousand employees and passed a hundred million units a year, building the modems, PON terminals and home gateways that carry other companies' logos. If you are sourcing networking hardware, the ODM layer is where the capability actually sits.

Now the gate, which is the most consequential single event documented anywhere in this guide. On 23 March 2026 the United States Federal Communications Commission added all consumer-grade routers produced in a foreign country to its Covered List, implementing a national security determination made three days earlier by a White House-convened interagency body. The practical effect is that new models cannot obtain FCC equipment authorisation, and without that they cannot be imported, marketed or sold in the United States. Routers already deployed are not banned or recalled and may continue to receive software and firmware updates, and an exemption route exists through Department of War or Homeland Security approval.

Read the definition carefully, because it is what makes this different from every other restriction in this atlas. A router counts as foreign-produced if any major stage of production occurs abroad, including manufacturing, assembly, design or development, and the location of final assembly is explicitly not determinative. Every previous trade measure could be answered by moving a production line. This one cannot: shifting final assembly to Vietnam does not help if the engineering happens in Nanshan. It is the first rule in this guide aimed at the design rather than the factory, which is precisely the layer this guide is about.

Europe went the other way and regulated the product rather than its origin. Since 1 August 2025 the cybersecurity provisions of the Radio Equipment Directive, Article 3(3)(d), (e) and (f) under Delegated Regulation (EU) 2022/30, have been mandatory, with EN 18031-1 the harmonised standard for anything acting as a network boundary. For a router that means demonstrated network protection, monitoring and anomaly reporting, and traffic controls such as rate limiting, and market surveillance authorities are actively checking. The Cyber Resilience Act reporting duty from September 2026 described on the Android tablets page sits on top. So the two largest markets now ask incompatible questions: one about where you are, the other about what your firmware does. Diligence follows from that. Ask where design and development actually take place rather than where the boxes are packed, ask to see the EN 18031 assessment rather than a CE certificate, and ask how long firmware support runs and how disclosed vulnerabilities are handled.

02 / Solution houses

The solution houses

01

GL.iNet 广联智通

Shenzhen · Hong Kong

Start here, because this is the house that answers the diligence question above rather than deflecting it. Founded 2010, operating through Shenzhen Guanglianzhitong Tech with headquarters at Pak Shek Kok in Hong Kong, a cloud team in Chengdu and offices in the United States, GL.iNet builds WiFi routers, IoT gateways and remote device management on OpenWrt. Every router runs it, with the company's own features layered on top rather than replacing it, which means the firmware can be read, audited, extended and if necessary replaced entirely, and the device does not die when a vendor loses interest. It sells into more than 120 countries and supplies Sonos, Toyota, AT&T, Schneider Electric and ExpressVPN, so this is not a hobbyist operation. Note the limit of the advantage, though: openness answers Europe's question about what the firmware does, and does nothing at all about America's question of where the design happens. An auditable router designed in Shenzhen is still a foreign-produced router.

02

TP-Link 普联技术

Shenzhen

The largest consumer WiFi brand in the world and the company at the centre of everything above. Its corporate structure is now part of the story: TP-Link Corporation Group began a formal separation from TP-LINK Technologies in China in 2022, and in October 2024 established TP-Link Systems Inc. in Irvine, California as global headquarters, owned by Jeffrey Chao and his wife. A Bloomberg investigation reported that the resulting American venture retained substantial operations in mainland China, and the company is currently contesting the FCC over exemption from the import ban. Whatever the outcome, every other house on this page is studying the structure, because corporate architecture has become a market-access strategy.

03

Tenda 腾达

Shenzhen

Shenzhen Jixiang Tenda Technology, founded 1999, holds a claim worth recording: it was the first company in China to build its own routers and wireless networking gear rather than assembling somebody else's. The range now runs well past retail WiFi into business networking, switches, broadband CPE and gateways, powerline adapters, mobile broadband and IP cameras, which is the standard shape of a mature Shenzhen networking house. It sits in the value tier below TP-Link internationally and is the more approachable of the two if you are looking for badge-and-ship arrangements rather than buying a brand's product.

04

T&W 共进电子

Shenzhen

Shenzhen Gongjin Electronics, trading internationally as T&W, was founded in 1991 and listed in Shanghai as 603118 in February 2015, and it is the hireable layer this guide exists to find. It is an OEM and ODM for networking and telecommunications hardware with production bases in Shenzhen and Shanghai, more than ten thousand employees and capacity past a hundred million units a year, building xDSL and VDSL modems, EPON and GPON terminals, cable modems, 3G and 4G routers and dongles, powerline adapters, WiFi access points and home gateways. Almost none of it carries its name. If your internet provider gave you a router, there is a fair chance this is who built it.

05

Skyworth Digital 创维数字

Shenzhen

Founded 1997 and listed in Shenzhen as 000810, Skyworth Digital came up through set-top boxes, which is the neighbouring trade to carrier CPE and increasingly the same one: the operator that ships you a gateway usually ships you a video box too, and both now run the same class of Linux or Android stack. Its range spans broadcast, OTT, Android TV, IPTV and RDK boxes alongside XGS-PON and GPON optical terminals, routers, LTE and powerline products, convergence gateways and IoT terminals, with a further push into XR and automotive. The house to approach when the requirement is an operator-grade device with a managed firmware lifecycle rather than a retail product.
